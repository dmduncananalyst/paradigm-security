Running a Cash Heavy Dispensary in Los Angeles Means Living With a Target on the Door

Security guard licensing rules vary; confirm current California BSIS requirements before hiring.

Cash on site is not a choice most dispensaries get to make

An ordinary retailer can push most sales onto cards and bank transfers, keeping the cash in the building low. Dispensaries have never had that same freedom. Reporting on the industry has explained for years that many banks and card networks refuse cannabis business because cannabis remains banned at the federal level, so many shops have been forced to deal largely in cash. That history still shapes the daily reality inside stores today, even where some payment options have improved.

Cash changes the risk in a way product alone never would. Cash is untraceable once it is taken. It does not need to be fenced, discounted, or explained. A thief who knows a store collects cash all day, holds it through the evening, and moves it on a schedule that regulars can observe does not need inside information to see the opportunity. He only needs to watch the store for a few days.

The amounts are not trivial. One widely reported industry estimate put the cost of a single dispensary robbery at more than $250,000 once cash, product, damage, and lost business were counted. Even a smaller loss can cripple a shop that operates on thin margins, pays high taxes and compliance costs, and cannot easily borrow to recover.

The pattern of attacks in California is organized, not random

California operators have described waves of coordinated break ins rather than isolated shoplifting. Cannabis Law Report described groups arriving in multiple cars, with as many as 20 people in masks involved in some incidents, and 15 legal cannabis operations broken into in the Oakland and Bay Area in a single week during one surge. Operators across the state estimated millions of dollars in product and cash taken in a short period, and shootouts between guards and thieves were reported as well.

Los Angeles has its own record. Crosstown LA, analyzing police data, reported that in the first nine months of 2023, 61 percent of crime reports at medical and recreational cannabis dispensaries in the city were for burglary or attempted burglary, alongside 19 robberies and one attempted robbery. Downtown recorded 13 crime reports at cannabis shops in that period, more than any other neighborhood, with Van Nuys next at nine.

More recent research shows the human side of that pattern. A 2025 UCLA Labor Center report on the California cannabis workforce, based on more than 1,100 worker surveys, found that nearly one third of workers had experienced robberies or burglaries. When a third of the people working in an industry say they have lived through a robbery or a break in at work, the risk cannot honestly be described as rare.

The danger falls first on the people behind the counter

Owners sometimes talk about robbery as a property loss, but employees experience it as a threat to their lives. A budtender, a receptionist checking IDs, or a manager closing alone at night is the person facing the masked group at the door or the individual who waits until the store empties near closing time. Staff did not sign up to judge in a split second whether a threat is real, whether to resist, or how to protect a customer standing next to them.

That fear does not end when an incident ends. Workers who have been through one robbery often expect another, and some leave the industry entirely. Hiring and training replacements in a regulated business is slow and expensive, and a store known locally as an easy or dangerous target struggles to keep experienced staff. The UCLA report also found that 85 percent of respondents wanted stronger health and safety protections, a number that tells owners how their own teams judge the current situation.

A break in hurts long after the shelves are restocked

The visible loss is only the beginning. A smashed storefront, broken locks, and damaged display cases have to be repaired before the store can reopen, and every closed day is revenue that never comes back. Insurance, where a shop can get it at all, often becomes more expensive after repeated incidents, and some owners quoted in Los Angeles reporting said frequent break ins and attempted robberies were driving premiums up for operators already struggling to find coverage.

Why ordinary retail security thinking falls short here

A clothing store or a coffee shop can treat theft mainly as shrinkage. A dispensary cannot, because the combination of cash, concentrated product value, and a product that moves easily on the illicit market makes the store worth a planned attack rather than an opportunistic one. Attackers who arrive in groups, in multiple vehicles, with tools for breaking locks are not deterred by the same things that discourage a lone shoplifter.

Relying on untrained staff to fill the gap is its own danger. Asking a budtender or a shift lead to double as security puts that employee in confrontation without the training, background check, or legal standing the role requires. In California, a company providing security must hold a Private Patrol Operator license through the Bureau of Security and Investigative Services, and individual guards must hold a valid guard card after background checks and required training. A shop that improvises its security with whoever is on shift carries the incident risk and the compliance risk at the same time.

This is why dispensary security in California is a licensed, specialized conversation rather than an add on duty. Cash transport, asset protection, and a visible professional presence are established service categories in this industry precisely because the underlying problem is structural. It does not disappear in a good month, and it does not depend on whether a particular store has been hit yet. Stores that have not been robbed are not stores that criminals have overlooked forever. In many cases, they are simply stores that have not been tested yet.
