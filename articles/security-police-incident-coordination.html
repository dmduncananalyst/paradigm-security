Private Security and Police Coordination During an Incident

When police attend an incident at a property with private security, there can be several different accounts of what happened. A guard may have observations from the location. Employees and visitors may have additional information. Police have their own responsibilities. The value of coordination is an accurate account and a clear understanding of roles, rather than a private team promising to direct a public investigation.

An incident has more than one perspective

A security guard may see the beginning of a disturbance while a store employee sees what happened inside a business. Another person might arrive only after the incident has changed. These perspectives can complement one another, but they should not be blended into an invented firsthand account.

The distinction between observation and information received from somebody else matters. It helps explain why a professional account should be factual rather than dramatic. A confident description is not necessarily a complete description. Uncertainty about timing, identity, or sequence should remain uncertainty until it is supported, rather than being filled in to make a report sound decisive.

Emergency assistance remains an independent need

National 911 guidance describes emergencies as situations requiring immediate assistance from police, fire, or ambulance services. A guard at the property does not remove the need for those services. Immediate danger and injuries must not be treated merely as internal business matters.

Private security can be involved in the property’s response while public responders address the responsibilities of their agencies. The two roles may exist at the same time. The public should not be told that all incidents must pass through a company before police can be contacted. In an emergency, the appropriate route is 911.

Security information does not decide criminal charges

An incident report from a property is not a court judgment. It may describe conduct, observations, and relevant information, but the label attached by a business does not determine whether someone will be charged or found guilty. This is an important boundary when a company reviews an event publicly.

For example, a property may describe a person as having violated its rules. That description and the legal assessment of a crime are different matters. Responsible communication avoids promising an arrest, a prosecution, or a particular case outcome. The value of professional documentation is clarity, not the appearance of controlling the criminal process.

Coordination is not a grant of police powers

The BSIS manual covers the relationship between security personnel and peace officers and emphasizes the different responsibilities of those roles. Ordinary private security does not gain public law enforcement authority simply because police attend a location.

Likewise, a friendly relationship between a security provider and individual officers is not a published guarantee of preferential treatment or faster emergency service. Businesses should be cautious about claims suggesting a private company can dictate police decisions. Describing lawful cooperation is appropriate. Claiming control over dispatch, investigations, or criminal outcomes would be a materially different representation and would require a basis the private assignment itself does not supply.

The client needs an accountable explanation

After an incident, a client may need to understand what was observed, what the company’s involvement was, and which matters are now with public authorities. That explanation should recognize the difference between property responsibilities and law enforcement responsibilities. It should also avoid publishing sensitive operational details.

A clear understanding of these boundaries helps a client know where private security’s responsibility ends. A private consultation is the appropriate place to review the circumstances of an assignment. The aim is a realistic understanding of the relationship between the parties, including what private security can document and what remains outside a provider’s control. Responsible expectations are more useful than promises of a predetermined outcome.
